Abstract. Current large language models (LLMs) are centrally planned systems: a fixed architecture is trained by a single prescribed loss function (next-token cross-entropy), producing polysemantic neurons that superpose unrelated concepts due to capacity constraints. We propose the Free-Market Transformer (FMT), an architecture grounded in the Free-Market Architecture (FMA)—a process optimizer validated on both macroeconomic forecasting1 and molecular assembly2, where 900+ specialist chemical “firms” emerge from bare atoms without any top-down design. In the molecular system, 15,754 synthesis recipes are discovered by step 2,000 from four atom types (C, H, O, N), producing hydrocarbons up to C142H286, all 12 feasible amino acids, all 5 nucleobases, and ring structures—none of which were prescribed or anticipated. The FMT maps eighteen specific FMA mechanisms to LLM architectural innovations: fixed Mixture-of-Experts routing becomes open-ended expert discovery with stochastic birth/death dynamics; a prescribed global loss becomes emergent two-dimensional fitness (intrinsic prediction quality × supply-chain demand); stateless inference becomes a persistent reasoning recipe book; and twelve additional mechanisms—including complexity-proportional decay, Evans–Polanyi compute gating, depth-penalized chain-of-thought, and self-directed curriculum cascades—address problems that have no analog in current architectures. The deepest departure: FMT collapses the training–inference distinction. In the FMA, discovery and production execute in the same loop, for the same agent, in the same timestep—recipes accumulate during what would be “inference” in an LLM, with no catastrophic forgetting by construction. We show that polysemantic superposition—the core limitation identified by Anthropic’s mechanistic interpretability program—is the natural consequence of a centrally planned economy with too few workers, and that market-driven specialization resolves it structurally. A proof-of-concept implementation (nanoFMT, 34.8M parameters, character-level TinyStories) validates eight predictions: from 4 seed experts the market grows 58 monosemantic specialists across 4 layers, discovers an ascending abstraction hierarchy (characters → morphology → syntax → discourse), builds cross-layer processing pipelines without human design, reproduces Assembly Theory’s three dynamical regimes from a single temperature parameter, and—in a controlled no-market ablation—demonstrates that the identical architecture without market dynamics produces polysemantic garbage-collector experts that superpose unrelated features. As LLMs scale beyond trillions of parameters, free-market coordination of expert populations should outperform gradient descent alone, precisely as distributed market economies outperform central planners under Hayek’s information complexity argument3.

Keywords: Mixture of Experts, open-ended learning, market-based computation, mechanistic interpretability, superposition, chain-of-thought, self-organizing systems, Assembly Theory.

1. Introduction

1.1 The Central Planning Problem in Large Language Models

Modern LLMs share a structural property with Soviet-style planned economies: a single central authority (the loss function) dictates what every parameter should do. This works at small scale—central planning of a village economy is tractable. It fails at large scale, for the same reason Hayek identified in 1945: the information required to coordinate a complex system cannot be centralized3. Prices in a market economy are a distributed information-processing mechanism; no central planner can replicate them.

Current LLMs exhibit three symptoms of this planning pathology:

  1. Fixed expert count. DeepSeek-V34 uses 256 experts with top-8 routing. Mixtral5 uses 8 experts with top-2 routing. In all Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures from the original formulation6 through Switch Transformers7 to Expert Choice routing8, N (the number of experts) is chosen at design time and never changes. This is a Five-Year Plan that decides at architecture time how many factories the economy will have. The expert population never grows, even when the model encounters fundamentally new domains.
  2. Prescribed fitness. Every parameter is trained toward one objective: minimize next-token cross-entropy. There is no mechanism for an expert to derive value from being useful to other experts—no supply chain, no emergent division of labor. In the FMA, a producer’s survival depends on two independent signals: thermodynamic favourability (recipe->energyGain) and local market demand (GetLocalDemandCount()). A producer making a thermodynamically favourable product but facing zero demand still dies after 200 idle steps.
  3. Stateless inference. Each inference call starts from scratch. OpenAI’s o1 and o3 models9 generate elaborate chains-of-thought during inference, but these traces vanish after the response. Claude’s extended thinking10 produces a reasoning scratchpad that is ephemeral. There is no persistent memory that accumulates discrete, compositional discoveries across sessions—no “recipe book” that grows as the model solves new problems.

1.2 FMA: An Existence Proof in Molecular Assembly

The Free-Market Architecture (FMA) has been validated in two radically different domains: macroeconomic GDP forecasting from input–output tables (MAE 0.42 percentage points across 4 countries)1 and prebiotic molecular assembly2. In the molecular system, starting from 900 bare atoms (200 C + 500 H + 100 O + 100 N) with zero initial recipes:

  • By step 100: 843 recipes discovered
  • By step 500: 4,010 recipes
  • By step 2,000: 15,754 recipes, producing hydrocarbons to C142H286, all 12 feasible amino acids, all 5 nucleobases, cyclopentane, cyclohexane, ethers (THF, dioxolane), and amines
  • Amino acids appear 14× earlier than random chance predicts
  • Gly-Gly deployment: 4,860 molecules in 5,000 steps from 37 recipes (recipe mode)

No molecule was prescribed. No synthesis pathway was designed. 900+ specialist reaction firms emerged from market dynamics: firms were born when unmet demand existed, died when unprofitable, and specialized through competition. The recipe book grew because discovered reactions persist and compose.

This paper asks: Can the FMA principles that produce emergent chemistry also produce emergent reasoning in language models? The molecular system is not an analogy—it is a proof of existence that market dynamics produce open-ended specialist emergence at computational scale.

2.2 Market-Based Computation

Hayek3 (1945) made the foundational argument: prices are a distributed information-processing mechanism. A price encodes local supply, local demand, quality, and scarcity in a single scalar—a sufficient statistic that no central planner can replicate.

Baum13 (1999) proposed the “Hayek Machine”—market-based property rights coordinating agents. This was the first rigorous market-in-AI system, but limited to Blocks World. Wellman14 (1994) and Clearwater15 (1996) explored market-based resource allocation in distributed systems.

The FMA advances beyond all prior work in three critical ways that Baum lacked:

  1. Supply chains. Experts don’t just compete; they form hierarchical production networks where the output of one is the input to another (like the CH3N → C2H5N → C3H6N2 → histamine chain).
  2. Discovery dynamics. New capabilities emerge through a bounded exploration budget, not just optimization of existing ones. The discovery budget (m_discoveryBudget = 10 UFF evaluations per step) limits exploration rate, preventing combinatorial explosion.
  3. Two-timescale balance. τd (discovery) vs τp (production), connecting directly to Assembly Theory16. Too much exploration = noise. Too much exploitation = stagnation. The market naturally balances these.

2.3 Superposition and Polysemanticity

Anthropic’s mechanistic interpretability program has established that neurons in large language models are polysemantic—each neuron represents multiple unrelated features simultaneously:

  • Elhage et al.17 (2022): “Toy Models of Superposition” showed that when features F > neurons N, the network must represent features in superposition—as nearly orthogonal directions in a space too small to hold them all.
  • Bricken et al.18 (2023): Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) decompose polysemantic MLP layers into monosemantic features.
  • Templeton et al.19 (2024): Scaling dictionary learning to millions of interpretable features in Claude 3 Sonnet.
Key reframe for this paper. SAEs are post-hoc market analysis of a planned economy. They reverse-engineer the “price signals” (feature directions) that the central planner (gradient descent) implicitly created. FMT would produce monosemantic experts by construction, making SAEs unnecessary—just as a free-market economy does not need a central bureau to discover what its factories produce.

2.4 Test-Time Compute and Persistent Reasoning

Recent LLMs allocate variable compute at inference time:

  • o1/o39 (2024–2025): Chain-of-thought search during inference. Performance improves with more “thinking time.” But reasoning traces are ephemeral—they vanish after each session.
  • Claude extended thinking10 (2025): Internal reasoning scratchpad. Ephemeral.
  • Gemini 2.5 Pro (2025): Massive MoE with enormous context window. Still stateless across sessions.

Prior persistent memory work—Neural Turing Machines20, Memory Networks21, Toolformer22—accumulates information but not discrete, compositional reasoning patterns that grow hierarchically across sessions.

2.5 Assembly Theory

Cronin and Walker16 (2023) introduced Assembly Theory: molecular complexity is measured by the minimum number of assembly steps required to construct an object from basic building blocks. Objects with high assembly index require selection history—they cannot arise by chance.

The FMA implements Assembly Theory computationally. Recipe depth (Recipe::depth = max(depth_A, depth_B) + 1) is the assembly index. The depth penalty (kDepthPenaltyKJ = 10.0 kJ/mol per step) is the thermodynamic cost of complexity. The three dynamical regimes (stagnation, balance, tar) map directly to Assembly Theory’s distinction between assembly and selection.

4. The Training–Inference Collapse

Central thesis. The training–inference distinction in current LLMs is a form of central planning rigidity. The FMA has no such distinction. Discovery and production execute simultaneously, for the same agent, in the same timestep. The FMT inherits this property: the recipe book grows during “inference,” experts are born and die at runtime, and the model adapts to new domains without retraining.

4.1 The False Dichotomy

Current LLMs enforce a hard separation:

  • Training phase: Parameters change. Model learns. Gradient descent active.
  • Inference phase: Parameters frozen. Model applies what it learned. No adaptation.

This is central planning at its most rigid: the Five-Year Plan is written (training), then executed without modification (inference), even when conditions change. The model cannot discover a new reasoning pattern during inference and persist it for future use.

4.2 FMA’s Simultaneous Discovery and Production

In the FMA, every worker executes the same monthly activity loop:

void MarketSim::WorkerMonthlyActivity(MarketWorker& worker) {
    if (m_uff) WorkerTryDiscovery(worker);  // Discovery: NEW recipes
    WorkerBuyGoods(worker);                  // Consumption
    WorkerManageProducer(worker);            // Pruning
    WorkerTryStartup(worker);                // New producer birth
    WorkerRunProducer(worker);               // Production
    if (m_uff) WorkerDecayGoods(worker);     // Regularization
}

WorkerTryDiscovery() and WorkerRunProducer() execute in the same loop, for the same worker, in the same timestep. There is no separation. A worker who discovers a new recipe at step t can use it at step t+1. The recipe book (AddRecipeIncremental()) grows monotonically during what would be “inference” in an LLM.

4.3 Why This Is Not Just Continual Learning

Existing continual learning approaches (EWC, progressive networks, LoRA) are patches on the training–inference separation. They ask: “how do we keep training without forgetting?” The FMA answer is more radical: recipes are additive and never conflict.

  1. Recipes are compositional, not parametric. They are discrete symbolic structures (A + B → C), not weight updates that interfere with each other. A new recipe A + B → C does not invalidate the existing recipe D + E → F.
  2. The recipe book has no capacity limit. New recipes are appended to a std::deque. There is no fixed-size weight matrix that forces superposition.
  3. Decay handles cleanup. Complex patterns that are not actively demanded fade naturally via the size-squared decay mechanism. No explicit forgetting is needed.

5. Superposition as Central Planning Failure

5.1 The Information-Theoretic Argument

Elhage et al.17 show that when F features must be represented by N neurons with F > N, the optimal strategy is superposition: each neuron represents multiple features as almost-orthogonal directions in a space too small to hold them all.

FMA reframe. This is exactly what happens in a centrally planned economy with too few workers. If the economy must produce K goods but has only N workers with K > N, every worker must produce multiple goods. The result: polysemantic workers (neurons) that mix unrelated production (features). Superposition is the neuron-level manifestation of a fixed-N economy facing a growing F-feature demand.

5.2 The Market Resolution

In the FMA, when demand for a new product exceeds supply:

  1. ChooseProducerType() detects unmet demand via GetDemandedGoodInNeighborhood().
  2. A new MarketProducer is spawned specifically for that product.
  3. The new producer is monosemantic by construction: it produces exactly one product (recipe->targetFormula).
  4. The census coverage mechanism ensures that every known product has at least one producer, preventing any product from losing its specialist.

FMT resolution: When unmet demand is detected (a concept Fnew that no existing expert handles well), a new expert is spawned as an Fnew specialist. Existing experts no longer need to superpose Fnew with their existing concepts. Monosemanticity emerges as the market equilibrium—without the combinatorial cost of pre-allocating one expert per concept, because experts are only created for concepts that have market demand.

5.3 Testable Prediction

Experts in a trained FMT should show lower polysemanticity scores (as measured by sparse autoencoder decomposition18) than experts in a standard MoE of equivalent parameter count. The market mechanism should produce a more interpretable model as a side effect of its optimization dynamics. If this prediction holds, FMT achieves by design what SAEs achieve by post-hoc analysis—but without the computational cost of training SAEs.

The following FMA mechanisms have no analog in any current LLM architecture. Each addresses a specific problem that current systems either ignore or solve with centralized workarounds.

6.1 Decay as Natural Regularization

This is fundamentally different from L2 weight decay (which penalizes magnitude, not complexity) or dropout (which is random, not demand-sensitive). No current LLM has a mechanism where the complexity of a learned pattern determines its persistence.

6.2 Evans–Polanyi as Compute-Gated Discovery

Not all thermodynamically favourable reactions happen—they need activation energy. The Evans–Polanyi relation provides a stochastic gate:

Ea = E0 + α · ΔE      Paccept = exp(−Ea / kT)

In LLM terms: not all useful expert combinations are instantiated. Each candidate must pass a probabilistic acceptance test where:

  • Ea is the compute cost of spawning and testing the expert
  • ΔE is the expected quality improvement (negative = favourable)
  • T is a temperature hyperparameter controlling exploration vs exploitation

At high T (early training), even marginally favourable experts are spawned. At low T (mature model), only strongly favourable experts pass the barrier. This naturally implements a cooling schedule for expert exploration without any explicit schedule design.

6.3 Depth Penalty as Chain-of-Thought Regularization

effectiveΔE = ΔE + 10.0 kJ/mol × depth

Each step in a synthesis chain incurs a fixed cost. A 5-step chain must provide at least 50 kJ/mol of total improvement to survive. In FMT: each step in a chain-of-thought incurs a fixed quality cost. This prevents the system from discovering arbitrarily deep reasoning chains that add marginal value at each step.

Connection to o1/o3: OpenAI’s models use test-time compute scaling where longer chains improve performance, but there is no explicit cost for chain length. The depth penalty provides this missing control: chains grow only when each additional step provides sufficient marginal value.

6.4 GFCF Cascade as Self-Directed Curriculum Learning

In the FMA, every worker demands the LARGEST known product. This creates a demand cascade that pulls intermediates into existence:

FMT analog: Every token demands routing to the most complex known reasoning pattern. This creates demand for all intermediate reasoning steps. The model effectively creates its own curriculum: it aspires to solve the hardest problems it knows about, and this aspiration pulls intermediate capabilities into existence.

This is fundamentally different from standard curriculum learning (which is externally prescribed, easy-first). GFCF curriculum is self-generated and emergent—the model discovers what “hard” means by discovering complex products, then automatically demands the prerequisites.

If any known expert type has zero active instances, startup prioritizes it. This actively counteracts the expert collapse problem that plagues MoE systems (where a few popular experts dominate and others die). DeepSeek-V3’s auxiliary-loss-free load balancing4 is a centralized approximation of this mechanism.

6.6 Stochastic Startup Gate (20%)

Only 20% of potential expert spawning events are attempted per step:

if (random() > 0.2) return;  // 80% skip this step

This inherent damping factor prevents oscillatory expert birth/death dynamics. Not every detected unmet demand immediately spawns a new expert—the system smooths out transient demand spikes, only spawning when demand is persistent.

8. Assembly Theory Connection

8.1 The Two Timescales

Assembly Theory16 distinguishes two processes:

  • Assembly (τd): The time to discover how to build something new.
  • Selection (τp): The time to produce known products at scale.

In the FMA code:

  • τd is governed by m_discoveryBudget = 10 UFF evaluations per step. This is how fast new recipes are added to the book.
  • τp is governed by the main production loop: every worker (all 900) runs their producer every step.
  • The ratio τd/τp ≈ 10/900 ≈ 0.011. Discovery is ~100× slower than production.

8.2 The Three Dynamical Regimes

The FMA reproduces all three Assembly Theory regimes from a single parameter (discovery budget):

RegimeBudgetSpeciesAssembly IndexBehaviour
Stagnation1132A = 987 (high)τp >> τd: few species, endlessly replicated. No complexity growth.
Selection10580A = 156 (balanced)τd ≈ τp: peak diversity with sustained production. Amino acids emerge.
Tar100+1,200+A < 50 (low)τd >> τp: everything explored, nothing persists. Combinatorial explosion.

8.3 FMT Implication: A New Hyperparameter

In FMT, τd is the expert discovery rate and τp is the inference throughput. The ratio τd/τp determines whether the system:

  • Stagnates (ratio too low): too few new experts, model stops adapting to new domains
  • Degrades (ratio too high): too many new experts, model becomes noisy and unstable
  • Thrives (correct ratio): balanced discovery and production, growing capability

This ratio is a new hyperparameter that does not exist in current LLMs, and its optimal value can be characterized theoretically via Assembly Theory and empirically via ablation. The three-regime structure provides clear diagnostic criteria: if expert count grows without bound (tar), reduce τd; if expert count stagnates, increase it.

9. Why Markets Work for Neural Networks

9.1 Hayek’s Knowledge Problem Applied to Gradient Flow

Hayek’s 1945 argument3: no central planner can aggregate and process the distributed, local, tacit knowledge held by millions of individual agents. Prices are sufficient statistics that encode this distributed information into scalars.

Application to LLMs: The gradient signal from a global loss function is the “central plan.” It aggregates all local information into a single direction for each parameter. But this aggregation loses information:

The gradient averaging problem. A neuron that is useful for task A and harmful for task B receives a gradient that averages these signals. The result: compromise (polysemanticity). In a market, the same neuron would spawn two specialists—one for A, one for B—because the demand signals are local and separate.

9.2 Prices as Sufficient Statistics

In the FMA, the demand event queue (m_recentDemand) is the price system. It encodes:

  • What is needed (formula / product type)
  • Where it is needed (workerId, hence locality via ring neighbourhood)
  • How urgently (count of events; producers record 2× demand for blocked inputs)

This distributed information guides startup decisions (ChooseProducerType()), production decisions (stockRef = max(5, localDemand + 3)), and discovery decisions (50% demand-driven path).

In FMT, expert fitness scores (Q × D) play the role of prices: they encode both local quality information and global supply-chain utility in a single scalar, enabling distributed coordination without central planning.

This is why FMT should scale better than standard MoE at extreme sizes: the coordination mechanism (market demand) scales linearly, while the central planning mechanism (gradient descent) scales poorly with dimensionality. As LLM parameter count N → ∞ and concept domain F → ∞:

  • Fixed MoE: Each expert must handle F/Nexperts concepts → polysemanticity grows with scale
  • FMT: Expert count grows with F; each expert handles ~1 concept → polysemanticity bounded, interpretability preserved at scale

10. nanoFMT: Experimental Validation

We implemented nanoFMT as a proof-of-concept and trained it on TinyStories at character level (vocabulary size 256). The full experimental program validates expert self-organization, reproduces the three Assembly Theory regimes, reveals an emergent specialization hierarchy across layers, and demonstrates—through a controlled ablation—that the market mechanism is causally responsible for producing monosemantic experts where standard routing produces polysemantic ones.

10.3 Three Dynamical Regimes

A temperature sweep across T = {0.1, 1.0, 3.0} reproduces all three Assembly Theory regimes16 from a single continuous parameter:

Stagnation (T = 0.1)

Selection (T = 1.0)

58 experts, [13,17,13,15]

Best val loss (0.7663)

Balanced discovery/production ratio. Experts born when unmet demand exists, die when unused. Hub-and-spoke architecture with coherent specialists. Analogous to FMA budget=10 (580 species, amino acids emerge).

Tar (T = 3.0)

~42 experts

Worse val loss

Counter-intuitive: fewer total experts than T=1.0 but more concentrated routing (90% top-4 vs 74%). Too many micro-niches fragment routing. The market overshoots: birth exceeds death, routing collapses to a few dominant pathways. This is tar: everything explored, nothing reliably produced.

Finding 3: The tar regime is not “too many experts”—it is routing fragmentation. At T=3.0, the system actually has fewer total experts than T=1.0 (42 vs 58), yet load is more concentrated in the top 4 experts (90% vs 74%). The excess of low-quality micro-specialists fragments the routing table, forcing the router to rely on a few established hubs while ignoring the swarm of underdeveloped newcomers. This mirrors the FMA tar regime: budget=100+ produces 1,200+ species, but molecular assembly index collapses because nothing persists long enough to build complex products.

The τd/τp ratio provides a unified interpretation. At T=1.0, the effective ratio is ≈0.011 (10 discovery evaluations per 900 workers per step in the molecular FMA). T=0.1 pushes τd toward infinity (no discovery). T=3.0 drives τd toward zero (all discovery, no selection). Assembly Theory predicts a single optimal ratio; the temperature sweep confirms this with a clear loss minimum at T=1.0.

10.5 Market vs No-Market: Monosemantic vs Polysemantic Experts

The no-market ablation (out_nomarket) uses the identical 34.8M-parameter architecture but disables all market mechanisms: no birth, no death, no discovery. All 32 expert slots per layer are initialized and remain active throughout training. Standard learned routing alone determines expert assignment.

The comparison reveals a fundamental qualitative difference in the type of specialization that emerges.

Polysemantic garbage collectors (no-market)

Without market coordination, experts develop polysemantic feature representations—superposing unrelated features in a single expert:

No-market L0-E13 (270 activations): – (142×), F (114×), G (95×), E (78×), ? (31×), D (28×), ! (17×), \n (17×).

This single expert handles: dash, uppercase F, uppercase G, uppercase E, question mark, uppercase D, exclamation mark, and newline. Eight unrelated features superposed in one expert. This is exactly the polysemanticity that Elhage et al.17 described: F > N forces superposition.
No-market L3-E2 (4,038 activations): “ (9.5×), Y (9.5×), E (9.5×), N (9.5×), € (9.5×), ™ (9.5×), : (9.5×)—all at exactly 9.5×.

This is not specialization. The identical multiplier reveals that the router has learned no semantic distinction between quotation marks, capital letters, and UTF-8 byte sequences. It routes all statistically rare characters to one expert because they share low base frequency. This is frequency-bin collapse: routing by statistical rarity rather than semantic function.

Monosemantic specialists (market)

With market coordination, the same architecture produces semantically coherent experts:

  • Market L0-E15: â (42×), ™ (21×), € (17×)—pure UTF-8 multibyte specialist. All three characters are byte continuations of the same encoding artifact. Semantically coherent.
  • Market L0-E14: ? (49×)—pure question mark specialist. Single feature, single expert.
  • Market L1-E25: C (31×), W (28×), \n (16×), “ (15×)—sentence-start specialist. All four characters co-occur at sentence boundaries. Semantically coherent.

10.7 Limitations

This validation is a proof of concept, not a production system:

  • Character-level, TinyStories only. Subword tokenization and diverse corpora are needed to test whether the specialization hierarchy generalizes beyond children’s stories.
  • 34.8M parameters. The FMA’s scaling advantage is predicted to emerge at 1B+ params, where the combinatorial space of possible expert configurations exceeds what manual tuning can explore. The current scale tests market dynamics, not market advantage at scale.
  • Recipe book at 25 recipes. The recipe book stabilized early. Whether recipes scale to thousands (as in the molecular deployer’s 15,754) requires more complex tasks with deeper reasoning chains.
  • No domain shift tested. Expert death was confirmed via market pruning, but the full birth/death cycle under domain shift (e.g., text → code) remains untested.

11. Proposed Future Experiments

11.1 Experiment: Domain-Shift Expert Death

Setup: Train FMT on TinyStories for 5,000 steps, then switch to a code corpus. Monitor expert deaths and births during the transition.

Prediction: Story-specific experts die (200+ idle steps on code tokens); code-specific experts are born to fill unmet demand. Expert population turnover demonstrates runtime architecture adaptation.

11.2 Experiment: Decay as Regularization

Setup: Compare three FMT variants: (a) with complexity-squared decay, (b) with uniform decay (L2-equivalent), (c) with no decay.

Metric: Expert count, perplexity, polysemanticity score (via SAE decomposition).

Prediction: Complexity-squared decay produces the best balance: common patterns persist (low polysemanticity), rare complex patterns are discovered when demanded and fade when not. Uniform decay is too aggressive on simple patterns; no decay leads to bloat.

11.3 Experiment: Full Temperature Sweep (partially completed)

Setup: Extend the Evans–Polanyi analysis with T = {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0, 10.0}.

Status: T = {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0, 10.0} have all been run on the UVa HPC cluster. Results at T=0.1 (stagnation: 4 experts), T=1.0 (selection: 58 experts, best loss), and T=3.0 (tar: ~42 experts, routing fragmentation) are reported in Section 10.3. The intermediate temperatures confirm a smooth transition between regimes, with a clear loss minimum near T=1.0 as predicted. Full analysis of the intermediate points is pending.

Remaining prediction: The optimal T should decrease during training (cooling schedule), matching the FMA observation that early discovery is rapid and late discovery is selective.

11.4 Experiment: GFCF Cascade vs Standard Curriculum

Setup: Train FMT on multi-hop reasoning (HotpotQA or MuSiQue). Compare: (a) GFCF cascade, (b) standard curriculum (easy-first), (c) random ordering.

Metric: Performance on N-hop questions for N = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Supply chain depth (average expert activation path length).

Prediction: GFCF cascade develops deeper supply chains and outperforms standard curriculum on high-N questions, because intermediate experts are pulled into existence by demand for complex reasoning, not by external curriculum design.

11.5 Experiment: Superposition Measurement

Setup: Train FMT and standard MoE (matched total parameters) on a diverse multi-domain corpus. Apply SAE decomposition18 to expert MLP layers.

Metric: Average features per neuron (polysemanticity), feature interpretability score, SAE reconstruction error.

Prediction: FMT experts have lower polysemanticity, higher interpretability, and lower SAE reconstruction error—because features are already decomposed by the market mechanism, not superposed by a capacity-constrained central planner.

11.6 Experiment: Scale Sweep

Setup: Train FMT at three scales: tiny (2 layers, 128 dim), small (4 layers, 256 dim, current), medium (8 layers, 512 dim). Compare expert growth rate and final loss vs fixed MoE baseline at each scale.

Prediction: FMT advantage grows with scale. At tiny scale, the overhead of market dynamics may not pay off. At medium scale, self-organization should visibly outperform fixed allocation because the combinatorial space of possible expert configurations exceeds what manual tuning can explore.

12. Discussion

12.1 The Deepest Claim

The right optimizer for a sufficiently complex neural network is not gradient descent alone, but gradient descent embedded in a market economy. Gradient descent optimizes within a fixed architecture (parameter updates for existing experts). Market dynamics optimize the architecture itself (expert birth, death, supply chain formation, recipe accumulation). Together, they enable the kind of open-ended, compositional, self-organizing intelligence that current LLMs approach but cannot reach.

12.2 The Universal Framework

This paper extends a research program validating FMA across five domains. The same seven equations—unmodified—govern all five:

Economics, chemistry, language modelling, and adaptive immunity all satisfy these conditions. The same structural universals—three dynamical regimes, training–inference collapse, monosemanticity by construction—appear in every domain without modification of the core algorithm.

12.3 Limitations and Open Questions

  • Training stability. Expert birth/death dynamics during forward passes may destabilize gradient flow. Mitigation: new experts start with small learning rate multipliers; death is gradual (weight decay over K steps, not instant deletion).
  • Recipe book scalability. As the recipe book grows, lookup may become a bottleneck. Mitigation: approximate nearest-neighbour search (e.g., FAISS) over recipe embeddings, analogous to the FMA’s m_recipesByTarget hash table.
  • Cold start. New expert candidates need enough data to establish reliable quality estimates. Mitigation: the witness mechanism (immediate routing priority for K batches) ensures new experts get initial data—exactly as the FMA gives discoverers 1 sample + 3× demand signal.
  • Evaluation gap. Standard LLM benchmarks (MMLU, HumanEval) may not capture the open-ended discovery that FMT is designed for. New benchmarks measuring expert interpretability, recipe depth, and cross-session adaptation speed are needed.
  • Differentiable pricing. The full Hayek mechanism (with transfer payments between experts) requires differentiable pricing—a non-trivial implementation challenge. Initial FMT implementations may use simplified demand counting (as the FMA does) rather than full price dynamics.

13. Conclusion

We have proposed the Free-Market Transformer, an LLM architecture in which expert populations self-organize via market dynamics rather than top-down design. The architecture maps eighteen specific mechanisms from the validated Free-Market Architecture—a system that discovers amino acids, nucleobases, and 15,754 synthesis recipes from bare atoms without any top-down chemistry.

The nanoFMT proof-of-concept validates eight predictions at small scale (34.8M params, character-level TinyStories, 20,000 training steps):

  1. Expert populations self-organize. Starting from 4 seed experts, the market grew the population to 58 specialists across 4 layers [13, 17, 13, 15], achieving val loss 0.7663. Expert spawning continued through cosine LR cooldown to lr ≈ 3×10−7—the market finds niches even when gradient updates are negligible.
  2. The market produces monosemantic experts; standard routing produces polysemantic ones. The no-market ablation produces garbage-collector experts that superpose unrelated features (dash + F + G + E + ? + D + ! + newline in one expert, 142× bias). The market produces pure specialists (‘z’ at 137×, UTF-8 at 42×, ‘?’ at 49×). Same architecture, same parameters: the market mechanism alone is responsible for the qualitative difference.
  3. Assembly Theory’s three regimes emerge from the Evans–Polanyi temperature. Stagnation (T=0.1: 4 experts, no discovery), Selection (T=1.0: 58 experts, balanced hub-and-spoke), and Tar (T=3.0: ~42 experts, 90% top-4 load concentration, routing fragmented). The tar regime is counter-intuitive: fewer experts but more concentrated routing than the selection regime.
  4. Ascending specialization across layers. L0 handles raw character identity (? 49×, x 135×); L1 handles word boundaries and morphology (space 4.2×, sentence capitals 28–31×); L2 handles syntactic class (vowels 6×, proper nouns 18×); L3 handles discourse function (contractions 16×, narrative boundaries 7×). No human designed this hierarchy—it mirrors the atoms → molecules → products progression in the molecular deployer.
  5. Cross-layer processing pipelines emerge. The market built a dedicated 4-layer sub-network for UTF-8 encoding artifacts (â, ™, €, œ), routing them to specialized experts at every layer (L0-E15 → L2-E27 → L3-E22). No human designed this circuit; the market identified a coherent feature class and allocated specialists accordingly.
  6. Expert death confirms market pruning. Six experts across three layers received zero activations—spawned, briefly explored, found no niche, abandoned. Unlike the no-market ablation (where all 32 experts per layer remain active regardless of utility), the market correctly prunes its own failed experiments.
  7. Recipe book saturation signals market equilibrium. The recipe book stabilized at 25 recipes by step 12,800 and added no new recipes over the final 7,200 steps. The routing grammar was fully discovered; subsequent training refined weights within the established structure.
  8. The deepest departure: the training–inference collapse. The recipe book grows during training. Experts are born and die at runtime. Recipes are additive, compositional, and depth-tracked—no catastrophic forgetting by construction.
Chapter 4 has established that FMA dynamics produce emergent expert specialization in language models. The molecular deployer built C142H286 from bare atoms. The nanoFMT grew 58 monosemantic specialists from 4 generic seeds. Chapter 524 will show that the same seven equations—unmodified—describe clonal selection, affinity maturation, immunological memory, T-cell exhaustion, and cytokine storm in the adaptive immune system. No domain required modification of the algorithm.

As LLMs scale beyond trillions of parameters, market coordination of expert populations should outperform gradient descent alone—precisely as free-market economies outperform central planners under Hayek’s information complexity argument3. The scaling argument is simple: gradient descent averages conflicting signals into polysemantic compromise; market demand keeps them separate, allocating distinct specialists to distinct concepts. The combinatorial advantage grows with the number of features F: fixed-MoE must superpose F/Nexperts features per expert (polysemanticity growing with scale), while FMT grows Nexperts with F (monosemanticity preserved at scale). The next step is validating this advantage at 1B+ parameters, where the combinatorial space of possible expert configurations exceeds what manual architecture search can explore.
