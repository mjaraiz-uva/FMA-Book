Large Language Models: A Technical Exposition

A comprehensive examination of the architecture, training, and deployment of modern large language models, designed for technically sophisticated practitioners.

1. What is a Language Model?

A language model is a probabilistic function that assigns a probability to a sequence of tokens. Formally, given a sequence of tokens $t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_n$, a language model computes the joint probability distribution:

$$P(t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(t_i \mid t_1, \ldots, t_{i-1})$$

This decomposition—known as the chain rule of probability—is foundational to language modeling. Each term represents the conditional probability of the next token given all preceding tokens, a quantity called the context.

In the context of neural language models, this distribution is parametrized by a neural network with parameters $\theta$. The training objective is to minimize the negative log-likelihood across a corpus of text:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log P_\theta(t_i \mid t_1, \ldots, t_{i-1})$$

Modern language models are autoregressive—they predict the next token conditioned on previous tokens. This enables both efficient training (via next-token prediction) and generation (via iterative sampling).

The utility of language models extends beyond mere sequence modeling. They serve as feature extractors, zero-shot learners, few-shot learners (in-context learning), and foundation models upon which task-specific capabilities can be built. The emergence of large language models (LLMs)—models trained on hundreds of billions of tokens with billions to hundreds of billions of parameters—has demonstrated unexpected capabilities in reasoning, code generation, and instruction-following.

2. From N-grams to Neural Networks

Historical language modeling began with n-gram models, which assigned probabilities based on fixed-length histories. An n-gram model uses the Markov assumption to approximate the conditional probability:

$$P(t_i \mid t_1, \ldots, t_{i-1}) \approx P(t_i \mid t_{i-n+1}, \ldots, t_{i-1})$$

N-gram models rely on maximum likelihood estimation from observed counts in training text. While computationally efficient and theoretically grounded, n-grams suffer from catastrophic sparsity—most n-gram sequences do not appear in training data, yielding zero probability estimates. Smoothing techniques (Laplace smoothing, Kneser-Ney smoothing) partially address this, but the fundamental limitation persists: the model cannot generalize beyond fixed context windows.

Neural language models revolutionized the field by learning distributed representations. Rather than explicit counting, they learn to approximate probability distributions through gradient-based optimization of neural architectures. The key innovation was the realization that learning word representations jointly with the language modeling objective yields superior generalization.

Early neural models employed recurrent neural networks (RNNs), specifically long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, to handle variable-length contexts. While RNNs theoretically accommodate arbitrary context, they suffer from vanishing and exploding gradients during backpropagation through time (BPTT), limiting effective context to roughly 50-100 tokens in practice.

// N-gram probability estimation pseudocode function ngramProbability(tokens, n): history = tokens[-(n-1):] count_history_next = countOccurrences(history + next_token) count_history = countOccurrences(history) return (count_history_next + smoothing) / (count_history + smoothing * vocab_size) // Neural approach: Learn continuous representations function neuralLMProbability(tokens, theta): representations = embedTokens(tokens, theta) context_repr = processWithNN(representations, theta) logits = projectToVocab(context_repr, theta) return softmax(logits)

The transition from n-grams to neural models represented a fundamental shift: from explicit statistical counting to implicit learning of probability distributions through high-dimensional geometry.

Tiny worked example: bigram (n=2) on a 3-word corpus Corpus: "the cat sat the cat ran" Vocabulary: {the, cat, sat, ran} Bigram counts (previous → next): the → cat: 2 cat → sat: 1 sat → the: 1 cat → ran: 1 Probabilities: P(cat | the) = 2/2 = 1.0 (after "the", always "cat") P(sat | cat) = 1/2 = 0.5 (after "cat", 50% "sat") P(ran | cat) = 1/2 = 0.5 (after "cat", 50% "ran") Generate from "the": the → cat → sat (or ran, coin flip) Problem: P(dog | the) = 0 — any unseen word gets zero probability. This is the sparsity problem that neural models solve.

3. Word Embeddings and Vector Spaces

A word embedding is a learned, continuous vector representation of a discrete token. Rather than representing words as atomic symbols, embeddings encode semantic and syntactic information in a high-dimensional vector space. For a vocabulary of size $V$ and embedding dimension $d$, each token is mapped to $\mathbb{R}^d$.

The embedding matrix $E \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}$ is a learnable parameter. During forward propagation, the embedding lookup is a simple indexing operation: given token index $i$, retrieve row $E_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$.

Embeddings are learned end-to-end as part of the language modeling objective, though they can also be initialized from pre-computed vectors (GloVe, Word2Vec). The remarkable property of learned embeddings is that geometric relationships in embedding space correspond to semantic relationships in language. The canonical example is the analogy: $\text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman} \approx \text{queen}$.

Modern LLMs typically use large embedding dimensions (768–12,288). This high dimensionality enables fine-grained representation of meaning but also introduces a parameter bottleneck. Token embeddings constitute a substantial fraction of model parameters, particularly for large vocabularies.

Beyond token embeddings, position embeddings encode sequential position information. Absolute position embeddings assign learned vectors to each position: $E_{\text{pos}}[i] \in \mathbb{R}^d$. Relative position embeddings, conversely, encode distances between positions. Absolute embeddings are simpler but suffer when sequence length exceeds training length. Relative embeddings generalize better to longer sequences.

Tiny worked example: embedding lookup (d=4, V=4) Vocabulary: {the=0, cat=1, sat=2, on=3} Embedding matrix E (4 words × 4 dimensions, learned values): E[0] = [0.2, -0.1, 0.8, 0.3] ← "the" E[1] = [0.9, 0.7, -0.2, 0.1] ← "cat" E[2] = [-0.3, 0.4, 0.6, -0.5] ← "sat" E[3] = [0.1, -0.3, 0.2, 0.9] ← "on" Input: "the cat" → token IDs [0, 1] Lookup: E[0], E[1] → [[0.2, -0.1, 0.8, 0.3], [0.9, 0.7, -0.2, 0.1]] Similarity: dot(E["cat"], E["sat"]) = 0.9×(-0.3) + 0.7×0.4 + (-0.2)×0.6 + 0.1×(-0.5) = -0.16 dot(E["cat"], E["the"]) = 0.9×0.2 + 0.7×(-0.1) + (-0.2)×0.8 + 0.1×0.3 = -0.02 In a trained model, semantically related words would have high dot products.

4. The Attention Mechanism

The attention mechanism computes a weighted aggregation of input representations. Given a query $q \in \mathbb{R}^d$, a set of keys $K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, and corresponding values $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, attention computes:

$$\text{Attention}(q, K, V) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i V_i \quad \text{where} \quad \alpha_i = \frac{\exp(q \cdot K_i^T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} \exp(q \cdot K_j^T)}$$

The numerator $q \cdot K_i^T$ measures alignment—the dot product between query and key. The softmax denominator normalizes attention weights to form a probability distribution. This mechanism is differentiable, enabling gradient-based learning of what to attend to.

Multi-head attention applies this mechanism in parallel across multiple representation subspaces:

$$\text{MultiHead}(Q, K, V) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \ldots, \text{head}_h) W^O$$ where $$\text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(Q W_i^Q, K W_i^K, V W_i^V)$$

Multiple heads enable the model to jointly attend to information from different representation subspaces, akin to different semantic aspects or syntactic features. In practice, models use 8-32 heads, each with dimension $d/h$.

A critical variant for autoregressive generation is causal attention (or masked attention), which prevents attending to future positions. The attention mask sets $\alpha_i = 0$ for all $i > j$ when computing attention for position $j$. This preserves the autoregressive property during training and generation.

Tiny worked example: self-attention on 3 tokens (d=2, 1 head) Input embeddings X (3 tokens × 2 dims): x₁ = [1.0, 0.0] ← "the" x₂ = [0.0, 1.0] ← "cat" x₃ = [0.5, 0.5] ← "sat" For simplicity, Q = K = V = X (no projection matrices). Step 1: Compute attention scores (dot products): score(x₁, x₁) = 1.0 score(x₁, x₂) = 0.0 score(x₁, x₃) = 0.5 score(x₂, x₁) = 0.0 score(x₂, x₂) = 1.0 score(x₂, x₃) = 0.5 score(x₃, x₁) = 0.5 score(x₃, x₂) = 0.5 score(x₃, x₃) = 0.5 Step 2: Apply causal mask (set future positions to -∞): Position 1 sees: [1.0, -∞, -∞ ] → only attends to itself Position 2 sees: [0.0, 1.0, -∞ ] → attends to positions 1-2 Position 3 sees: [0.5, 0.5, 0.5] → attends to all positions Step 3: Softmax row-wise: α₁ = [1.00, 0.00, 0.00] ← "the" looks only at itself α₂ = [0.27, 0.73, 0.00] ← "cat" mostly at itself, some at "the" α₃ = [0.33, 0.33, 0.33] ← "sat" attends equally to all three Step 4: Weighted sum of values: out₁ = 1.00×[1,0] = [1.00, 0.00] out₂ = 0.27×[1,0] + 0.73×[0,1] = [0.27, 0.73] out₃ = 0.33×[1,0] + 0.33×[0,1] + 0.33×[0.5,0.5] = [0.50, 0.50] Result: each position has gathered information from its context.
function scaledDotProductAttention(Q, K, V, mask=None): scores = matmul(Q, transpose(K)) / sqrt(d_k) if mask is not None: scores = scores + (mask * -1e9) weights = softmax(scores, dim=-1) output = matmul(weights, V) return output function multiHeadAttention(X, num_heads): Q = projectToQuery(X) K = projectToKey(X) V = projectToValue(X) heads = [] for h in range(num_heads): head_output = scaledDotProductAttention( Q[h], K[h], V[h], causal_mask ) heads.append(head_output) return linearProjection(concatenate(heads))

5. The Transformer Architecture

The Transformer, introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017), replaced recurrence entirely with attention and position-wise feed-forward networks. A Transformer block applies three distinct operations in sequence: multi-head self-attention, layer normalization, and a feed-forward network (FFN).

Self-attention applies the attention mechanism where queries, keys, and values all derive from the same input: $Q = K = V = X$. This enables each position to attend to all other positions (and itself) in the sequence.

Layer normalization stabilizes training by normalizing activations to zero mean and unit variance per sequence element:

$$\text{LayerNorm}(x) = \gamma \odot \frac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}} + \beta$$

The feed-forward network is a two-layer MLP applied position-wise:

$$\text{FFN}(x) = \max(0, x W_1 + b_1) W_2 + b_2$$

The hidden dimension of the FFN (typically 4d) provides capacity for nonlinear transformations. Residual connections—adding the input to the output—enable deep stacking of layers.

Tiny worked example: one Transformer block (d=2, FFN hidden=4) Input from attention: h = [0.27, 0.73] (for "cat" position) Step 1 — Layer Norm: normalize to zero mean, unit variance mean = 0.50, std = 0.23 h_norm = [(0.27-0.50)/0.23, (0.73-0.50)/0.23] = [-1.0, 1.0] Step 2 — FFN up-projection (d=2 → hidden=4): W₁ (2×4 matrix, learned): z = h_norm × W₁ + b₁ z = [-1.0, 1.0] × W₁ = [0.3, -0.8, 1.2, -0.1] Step 3 — ReLU activation: max(0, z) z_relu = [0.3, 0.0, 1.2, 0.0] ← two neurons "fire", two are silent Step 4 — FFN down-projection (hidden=4 → d=2): ffn_out = z_relu × W₂ = [0.15, -0.20] Step 5 — Residual connection: add input back output = h + ffn_out = [0.27 + 0.15, 0.73 + (-0.20)] = [0.42, 0.53] This is ONE block. GPT-2 stacks 12 blocks; GPT-3 stacks 96. Each block refines the representation a little more.

The Transformer is inherently parallelizable. Unlike RNNs, which must process sequences sequentially, Transformers compute attention over the entire sequence simultaneously. This enables efficient training on modern hardware (GPUs, TPUs) with batch processing and mixed precision.

For inference, however, the self-attention mechanism presents a computational challenge: computing attention over all previous positions has complexity $O(n^2)$ in sequence length, necessitating careful optimization techniques for long-context generation.

Tiny worked example: Mixture of Experts (MoE) replacing the FFN In a standard Transformer, the FFN applies the same weights to every token. In a MoE Transformer, each token is routed to a SUBSET of expert FFNs. Setup: 4 experts (E₀, E₁, E₂, E₃), top-k=2 (each token uses 2 experts) Input token: h = [0.42, 0.53] (output from attention layer) Step 1 — Gating network: linear projection h → 4 logits gate_logits = h × W_gate = [1.2, 0.3, -0.5, 0.8] Step 2 — Top-k selection (k=2): pick 2 highest Selected: E₀ (logit=1.2), E₃ (logit=0.8) Ignored: E₁ (0.3), E₂ (-0.5) ← these experts DON'T run Step 3 — Softmax over selected logits only: w₀ = exp(1.2) / (exp(1.2) + exp(0.8)) = 0.60 w₃ = exp(0.8) / (exp(1.2) + exp(0.8)) = 0.40 Step 4 — Run only the 2 selected experts: E₀(h) = [0.10, -0.15] (expert 0's FFN output) E₃(h) = [0.20, 0.05] (expert 3's FFN output) Step 5 — Weighted combination: output = 0.60 × [0.10, -0.15] + 0.40 × [0.20, 0.05] = [0.06, -0.09] + [0.08, 0.02] = [0.14, -0.07] Result: 4 expert FFNs = 4× the parameters, but only 2 run per token. → Dense model cost: 4 FFN forward passes per token → MoE cost: 2 FFN forward passes per token (50% compute savings) Mixtral 8×7B: 8 experts, top-2 → 47B params, ~13B active per token. This is why MoE models always operate with a FIXED, limited expert count.

6. Training: Pretraining and Fine-tuning

LLM training occurs in two phases: pretraining and fine-tuning. Pretraining involves unsupervised learning on massive text corpora (hundreds of billions to trillions of tokens). The objective is standard next-token prediction with cross-entropy loss:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log P_\theta(t_i \mid t_1, \ldots, t_{i-1})$$

Modern pretraining employs several techniques to enhance convergence and model quality:

Learning rate scheduling: Training typically uses a warmup phase (linear increase) followed by decay (cosine annealing). This balances initial stability with eventual refinement.

Gradient clipping: Clipping gradient norms prevents exploding gradients, maintaining stable training dynamics across very deep networks.

Weight decay (L2 regularization): Adds a penalty term $\lambda \|\theta\|_2^2$ to encourage smaller weights and mitigate overfitting.

After pretraining, fine-tuning specializes the model for downstream tasks. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) uses labeled examples for specific tasks (e.g., question answering, code generation). The loss remains cross-entropy, but computed over curated, high-quality datasets rather than raw web text.

Fine-tuning hyperparameters differ markedly from pretraining: smaller learning rates (1e-5 to 5e-5 versus 1e-4 to 1e-3), fewer training steps (thousands versus billions), and often selective unfreezing of layers to prevent catastrophic forgetting.

function pretrainingLoop(model, corpus, num_epochs): optimizer = AdamW(lr=1e-3) scheduler = CosineAnnealingWithWarmup(warmup_steps=10k) for epoch in range(num_epochs): for batch in corpus: tokens = tokenize(batch) logits = model(tokens[:-1]) loss = crossEntropy(logits, tokens[1:]) loss.backward() clipGradientNorm(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0) optimizer.step() scheduler.step() if step % 100 == 0: logPerplexity(loss) function finetuningLoop(pretrained_model, labeled_dataset): optimizer = AdamW(lr=5e-5) for epoch in range(3): for batch in labeled_dataset: tokens, labels = batch logits = pretrained_model(tokens) loss = crossEntropy(logits, labels) loss.backward() optimizer.step() optimizer.zero_grad()

7. Tokenization

Tokenization converts raw text into sequences of tokens suitable for neural processing. The choice of tokenization strategy profoundly impacts model behavior, training efficiency, and emergent capabilities.

Character-level tokenization treats individual characters as tokens. Vocabulary size is fixed and small (~256 for UTF-8), but sequences become long, increasing computational cost and making long-range dependencies harder to learn.

Word-level tokenization treats whitespace-separated words as tokens. While intuitive, word-level tokenization yields large vocabularies (50k–100k+) due to inflections, typos, and out-of-vocabulary issues. It also fails to capture subword morphology.

Subword tokenization balances these concerns. The dominant approach, byte-pair encoding (BPE), iteratively merges the most frequent adjacent token pairs in the training corpus. Given a corpus, BPE constructs a vocabulary by:

function byteePairEncoding(corpus, target_vocab_size): vocabulary = initializeCharacterVocab(corpus) pair_frequencies = {} while len(vocabulary) < target_vocab_size: pair_frequencies.clear() for sequence in corpus: pairs = getAdjacentPairs(sequence) for pair in pairs: pair_frequencies[pair] += 1 most_frequent_pair = argmax(pair_frequencies) mergeTokens(vocabulary, most_frequent_pair) updateCorpus(corpus, most_frequent_pair) return vocabulary
Tiny worked example: BPE on a tiny corpus Corpus: "low lower lowest" Initial character-level tokens: l o w _ l o w e r _ l o w e s t Step 1: Count adjacent pairs: (l,o)=3 (o,w)=3 (w,_)=1 (w,e)=2 (e,r)=1 (e,s)=1 (s,t)=1 Merge most frequent: (l,o) → "lo" Vocab: {l, o, w, e, r, s, t, _, lo} Corpus: lo w _ lo w e r _ lo w e s t Step 2: Count again: (lo,w)=3 (w,_)=1 (w,e)=2 (e,r)=1 (e,s)=1 (s,t)=1 Merge: (lo,w) → "low" Vocab: {..., low} Corpus: low _ low e r _ low e s t Step 3: Count: (low,_)=1 (low,e)=2 (_,low)=1 (e,r)=1 (e,s)=1 (s,t)=1 Merge: (low,e) → "lowe" Vocab: {..., lowe} Corpus: low _ lowe r _ lowe s t After 3 merges: "lower" = ["lowe", "r"], "lowest" = ["lowe", "s", "t"] Common stems are captured. Unknown word "lowing" → ["low", "i", "n", "g"].

BPE typically yields vocabulary sizes of 30k–100k tokens. Notably, the same BPE vocabulary learned on English text often tokenizes other languages inefficiently, a known limitation of monolingual tokenization.

Modern LLMs use variants like SentencePiece (designed for multilingual text) or WordPiece (used in BERT). These handle Unicode robustly and often include a pre-tokenization step to normalize text.

The relationship between tokenization and model behavior is subtle. Models must learn token-specific semantics from context. Some phenomena emerge from tokenization artifacts: for example, concatenations of tokens (e.g., "unwrap" as "un" + "wrap") can influence how models reason about morphology.

Detokenization (converting tokens back to text) requires careful handling of whitespace. Most modern tokenizers encode whitespace as an explicit symbol (e.g., "▁" in SentencePiece), enabling deterministic reconstruction.

8. Scaling Laws and Emergent Capabilities

Empirical scaling laws describe how model performance depends on training data size $N$, model parameters $P$, and compute budget $C$. Kaplan et al. (2020) and subsequent work established power-law relationships:

$$L(N) = aN^{-\alpha}, \quad L(P) = bP^{-\beta}, \quad L(C) = cC^{-\gamma}$$

Empirically, $\alpha \approx 0.08$, $\beta \approx 0.07$, and $\gamma \approx 0.17$ (where loss is measured in nats). These exponents suggest that doubling model size reduces loss by ~5%, while doubling data reduces loss by ~6%. Compute, however, reduces loss more efficiently when optimally allocated.

The compute-optimal frontier describes the allocation of fixed compute budgets between model size and data quantity. Chinchilla scaling suggests that for optimal performance, training compute should be allocated equally between model and data: $N \approx 20P$ (approximately 20 tokens per parameter). This represents a shift from earlier practice, which often under-trained large models.

Beyond scaling laws, models exhibit emergent capabilities—sudden jumps in performance on specific tasks as scale increases. Examples include in-context learning (few-shot adaptation from demonstrations in the prompt), chain-of-thought reasoning (breaking problems into intermediate steps), and arithmetic. These capabilities often appear discontinuous but likely reflect phase transitions in learned representations.

A critical observation: scaling laws hold reliably at large scale but do not extrapolate indefinitely. The assumption of infinite data and compute is physically implausible. Understanding the asymptotic behavior of scaling laws and identifying efficiency improvements (architectural innovations, training techniques) remains an active area.

9. From GPT to Modern LLMs

The GPT lineage exemplifies the evolution of LLM architectures and training paradigms. GPT-1 (2018) demonstrated that large-scale unsupervised pretraining on diverse text, combined with minimal task-specific fine-tuning, achieves strong performance on downstream NLP tasks. It trained a 12-layer Transformer on 7,000 diverse books.

GPT-2 (2019) scaled this to 1.5B parameters and 40GB of text (WebText). The authors showed that language models trained at scale exhibit zero-shot transfer: without any fine-tuning, GPT-2 performed reasonably on tasks like machine translation, summarization, and question answering, despite never being explicitly trained on them.

GPT-3 (2020) introduced two paradigm shifts: (1) few-shot in-context learning—providing task examples within the prompt enables the model to adapt to new tasks without parameter updates—and (2) massive scale: 175B parameters trained on 570GB of text. GPT-3 demonstrated that scale alone, without architectural innovations, yields remarkable capabilities.

Subsequent models refined this approach. Claude, Llama, and other modern LLMs employ architectural improvements (e.g., rotary positional embeddings, GQA—grouped query attention) and training techniques (e.g., instruction tuning, RLHF) to improve efficiency and controllability. Instruction tuning involves fine-tuning on prompt-response pairs where the prompt is a task description and the response is an ideal completion. This improves task adherence and generalization to unseen instructions.

A recent trend is open-source LLMs (Llama, Mistral, Qwen). While smaller than proprietary models (7B–70B vs. 100B+), they offer computational tractability and enable researchers to study LLM behavior in detail.

10. Inference: How Text is Generated

Inference (generation) iteratively samples or selects tokens from the model's predicted distribution. Given a prompt $p = t_1, \ldots, t_k$, the model computes $P(t_{k+1} \mid t_1, \ldots, t_k)$ over the entire vocabulary. Token selection determines generation quality and diversity.

Greedy decoding selects the highest-probability token at each step:

$$t_{i+1} = \arg\max_j P(t_j \mid t_1, \ldots, t_i)$$
Tiny worked example: greedy vs sampled generation Prompt: "The cat" Vocabulary: {sat, ran, slept, meowed, the, a, on} Model outputs probabilities for next token: P(sat)=0.35 P(ran)=0.25 P(slept)=0.20 P(meowed)=0.15 P(on)=0.05 Greedy: pick argmax → "sat" (always, deterministically) Temperature T=0.5 (sharpen): recompute softmax(logits / 0.5) P(sat)=0.52 P(ran)=0.24 P(slept)=0.14 P(meowed)=0.08 P(on)=0.02 → "sat" much more likely, almost deterministic Temperature T=2.0 (flatten): recompute softmax(logits / 2.0) P(sat)=0.24 P(ran)=0.22 P(slept)=0.20 P(meowed)=0.19 P(on)=0.15 → nearly uniform, any word could appear — creative but noisy Top-p (p=0.8): sort by prob, take smallest set summing to ≥ 0.8 {sat=0.35, ran=0.25, slept=0.20} → sum=0.80 ≥ 0.8 ✓ Sample only from these three. "meowed" and "on" excluded.

This is deterministic and fast but often produces repetitive, low-quality text. Greedy decoding fails to correct early mistakes, and the greedy path need not correspond to the globally highest-probability sequence.

Beam search maintains a set of $k$ hypotheses, expanding the most promising branches at each step. This finds higher-probability sequences than greedy decoding but is computationally expensive, scaling as $O(kb)$ where $b$ is vocabulary size.

Temperature-based sampling adjusts the distribution's sharpness before sampling. Given logits $z$, the sampled distribution is:

$$P(t) = \frac{\exp(z_t / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)}$$

Temperature $T > 1$ flattens the distribution (higher diversity), $T < 1$ sharpens it (lower diversity). $T = 1$ recovers the model's original distribution.

Top-k and nucleus (top-p) sampling restrict the sampling pool. Top-k sampling samples only from the $k$ most probable tokens, ignoring the long tail. Top-p (nucleus) sampling samples from the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds $p$. These techniques reduce pathological samples while preserving diversity.

A practical consideration: the computational cost of generation. The forward pass for each new token requires recomputing attention over all previously generated tokens, leading to $O(n^2)$ complexity in output length. Key-value caching mitigates this by caching computed keys and values, avoiding recomputation.

function generateWithSampling(prompt, model, max_tokens, temperature=1.0): tokens = tokenize(prompt) for _ in range(max_tokens): logits = model(tokens)[-1] // Last token's logits logits = logits / temperature probabilities = softmax(logits) next_token = sample(probabilities) // Stochastic sampling tokens.append(next_token) if next_token == END_OF_SEQUENCE: break return detokenize(tokens) function generateWithNucleusSampling(prompt, model, max_tokens, top_p=0.9): tokens = tokenize(prompt) for _ in range(max_tokens): logits = model(tokens)[-1] probabilities = softmax(logits) // Sort and compute cumulative probability sorted_probs, sorted_indices = sort(probabilities, descending=True) cumsum = cumsum(sorted_probs) // Find cutoff: smallest set whose cum prob exceeds top_p cutoff = cumsum <= top_p nucleus_probs = sorted_probs[cutoff] next_token = sample(nucleus_probs) tokens.append(next_token) return detokenize(tokens)

11. RLHF and Alignment

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) aligns language models with human preferences. The motivation is straightforward: models trained solely on next-token prediction may generate harmful, factually incorrect, or misaligned outputs. RLHF introduces a reward signal derived from human judgments.

RLHF proceeds in three stages:

Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). Fine-tune the pretrained model on a dataset of high-quality examples where the input is an instruction and the output is a desired response. This grounds the model in the desired behavior.

Stage 2: Reward Model Training. Collect pairs of model completions for the same prompt and obtain human preferences (pairwise comparisons: "which response is better?"). Train a reward model $R(x, y)$ to predict human preferences. The reward model is typically a language model with an additional regression head that outputs a scalar reward.

Stage 3: RL Policy Optimization. Use the reward model as a signal to optimize the policy (the language model) via reinforcement learning. The objective balances reward maximization with a constraint: the policy should remain close to the SFT model to prevent gaming the reward function or catastrophic forgetting.

The standard RL objective is:

$$\mathcal{J}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D, y \sim \pi(\cdot|x)}[R(x,y)] - \beta \cdot \text{KL}(\pi || \pi_{\text{SFT}})$$

The KL divergence term prevents the policy from diverging too far from the SFT model, preserving learned knowledge and preventing reward hacking. $\beta$ controls the trade-off.

In practice, the RL step uses Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), a stable policy gradient algorithm. PPO introduces a clipping mechanism to constrain the ratio of new and old policy probabilities, preventing excessively large updates.

A challenge with RLHF is scalability: obtaining large numbers of human preferences is expensive. Constitutional AI (CAI) offers an alternative: instead of human feedback, use a set of principles (constitution) and have the model evaluate its own outputs against these principles, creating a signal for fine-tuning.

// RLHF Policy Optimization (Proximal Policy Optimization) function ppoUpdate(model, reward_model, data, num_epochs): for epoch in range(num_epochs): for batch in data: prompts, completions_old = batch // Forward pass through current policy log_probs_new = model.logProb(completions_old) log_probs_old = model.logProb(completions_old).detach() // Compute advantages using reward model and baseline rewards = reward_model(prompts, completions_old) advantages = rewards - valueFunction(prompts) // PPO clipping: constrain probability ratio ratio = exp(log_probs_new - log_probs_old) clipped_ratio = clip(ratio, 1 - epsilon, 1 + epsilon) policy_loss = -min(ratio * advantages, clipped_ratio * advantages).mean() kl_loss = kldiv(model.logProb, sft_model.logProb) total_loss = policy_loss + beta * kl_loss total_loss.backward() optimizer.step()

12. Current Limitations and Open Problems

Despite remarkable progress, LLMs exhibit significant limitations. Understanding and addressing these limitations drives current research.

Context Length: Even large models have fixed context windows (typically 4k–100k tokens). This constraint limits the models' ability to reason over long documents, conduct multi-turn conversations with extensive history, or leverage extended in-context examples. Extending context length requires architectural innovations (e.g., sparse attention, hierarchical attention) and efficient attention implementations.

Hallucination: Models confidently assert factually incorrect information, especially for unfamiliar topics or recent events. This arises from the training objective (next-token prediction) which does not require correspondence to factual truth. Mitigation strategies include retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), fine-tuning on factual data, and uncertainty quantification.

Reasoning and Generalization: While large models exhibit in-context learning and few-shot adaptation, they often fail on problems requiring systematic reasoning or generalization beyond training distribution. For instance, models struggle with arithmetic on large numbers, logical inference chains of moderate length, and algorithmic tasks not covered by training data.

Interpretability: Understanding what computations occur inside large models remains largely intractable. Mechanistic interpretability—reverse-engineering learned circuits—is an active research area but remains in early stages. This limits our ability to diagnose failures, predict model behavior, and ensure safety.

Efficiency: Training and inference require massive computational resources. Modern LLMs demand specialized hardware (GPUs, TPUs) and consume substantial energy. Efficiency improvements (pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation, more compute-optimal training) are critical for accessibility and sustainability.

Multimodality and Embodiment: Current LLMs process text exclusively. Humans integrate language with vision, sound, and physical interaction. Vision-language models address multimodality but genuine integration of multiple modalities with reasoning remains limited. Embodied AI—integrating language with robotic control and environmental interaction—is nascent.

Alignment and Safety: Despite RLHF and constitutional AI, models can be adversarially manipulated, generate toxic content, or behave deceptively. Formal approaches to alignment (interpretability, verification, robust training) and adversarial robustness remain unsolved. The alignment problem becomes more acute as model capabilities increase.

Compositionality: Humans compose simple concepts into complex thoughts. Models struggle with compositionality: given trained sub-tasks, combining them into novel compositions is difficult. This limits transfer learning and few-shot generalization.

Theoretical Understanding: Despite empirical successes, theoretical understanding of why and how large models work remains limited. Scaling laws describe empirical relationships but lack rigorous derivations. The connection between next-token prediction and emergent reasoning capabilities is unclear.

The path forward likely involves hybrid approaches: combining neural methods with symbolic reasoning, integrating multiple modalities, developing new architectures beyond Transformers, and fundamentally rethinking the training paradigm to better align with human values and reasoning. These directions suggest that the LLM landscape will continue to evolve significantly in coming years.

This exposition represents the state of understanding as of early 2025. Language model research advances rapidly; readers are encouraged to consult recent publications for the latest developments.
