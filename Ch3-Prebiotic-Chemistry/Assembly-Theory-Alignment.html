Assembly Theory Alignment: Computational Evidence from a Darwinian Agent-Based Model

Martin Jaraiz — University of Valladolid — March 2026

1. Introduction

Assembly Theory (AT) proposes a framework for detecting selection in molecular systems by examining the relationship between structural complexity and abundance1. Its core constructs are:

  • Assembly index (ai): the minimum number of recursive joining steps needed to construct an object from basic building blocks.
  • Copy number (ni): how many copies of an object exist in a sample.
  • Formation history: the unique pathway of recursive construction steps that defines each object.

AT's central equation quantifies the assembly of an ensemble:

A  =  Σ  eai × (ni − 1)  /  NT

where the sum runs over all species, ai is the assembly index, ni the copy number, and NT the total object count. The exponential weighting means that even modest copy numbers of high-index objects contribute enormously to A.

AT's key prediction: High assembly index combined with high copy number is a signature of selection. Random (undirected) processes produce monotonically declining copy numbers as assembly index increases. Only selection can sustain or amplify high-index objects.

Our Darwinian Agent-Based Model (FM-ABM) provides an explicit mechanism for the selection that AT measures. Where AT detects selection after the fact, the FM-ABM implements it in real time through free-market dynamics: demand cascades, competitive pricing, and resource allocation by economic agents.

3. Experiment 1: Directed vs. Undirected Exploration

AT distinguishes between directed processes (with selection) and undirected processes (without selection). We test this by comparing two FM-ABM modes:

Directed (Full Mode)

GFCF demand active. The market selects for specific complex molecules, allocating resources toward their production.

Undirected (Free Mode)

No demand signal. Discovery and production proceed without any selection pressure — pure thermodynamic exploration.

5. The Non-Monotonic Copy Signature

The most striking result is the non-monotonic copy-number distribution in the Budget = 1 regime. Consider the data:

Depth123456789
Copies5301072911729143826
Key observation: Copies at depth 8 (avg = 38) exceed copies at depth 4 (avg = 11). Copies at depth 6 (avg = 29) match depth 3 (avg = 29). This reversal is impossible without selection — random combinatorial processes always produce monotonic copy-number decline with increasing assembly index.

AT provides the theoretical framework for this observation. Equation 2 in Sharma et al. describes the growth dynamics:

dNa+1/dt  =  kd × (Na)α     with   α < 1

When α < 1, the production rate of higher-assembly objects is sublinear in the abundance of their precursors. This creates a regime where selection can amplify specific high-index objects, producing exactly the non-monotonic pattern we observe.

Mechanism: In the FM-ABM, the free market achieves α < 1 through GFCF demand cascades. When final demand targets a complex molecule, intermediate products are consumed to produce it, creating selective amplification of specific high-depth products at the expense of generic low-depth ones. The market does not amplify all high-depth molecules — only those on the supply chains demanded by GFCF. This is precisely the selectivity that AT's α parameter captures.

6. Key Implications

6.1 First explicit mechanism for AT's selection parameter

The FM-ABM is, to our knowledge, the first computational system that provides an explicit, tuneable mechanism for Assembly Theory's selection parameter. AT measures selection after the fact by analyzing molecular ensembles; the FM-ABM implements selection in real time through economic agents that allocate resources, generate demand, and compete for profit.

6.2 Three regimes from a single parameter

AT's three τd/τp regimes — stagnation, selection, and tar — emerge naturally from varying discoveryBudget, a single integer parameter. No additional tuning is required. This confirms that AT's phase diagram reflects a fundamental property of combinatorial discovery under resource constraints, not an artifact of specific model assumptions.

The “best” regime for open-ended evolution is the balanced one, where both exploration and exploitation are sustained.

6.4 From measurement to mechanism

Assembly Theory provides a powerful analytical lens: given an ensemble of molecules, it can determine whether selection has acted. The FM-ABM complements this by providing the generative side: given a set of atoms and market rules, it produces the ensembles that AT can then analyze. Together, they form a complete loop from mechanism to measurement and back.
