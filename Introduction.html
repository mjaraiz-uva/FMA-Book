The Free-Market Algorithm — A Universal Theory of Open-Ended Complex Systems
Introduction

Why a Free Market?

Martin Jaraiz — Department of Electricity and Electronics, University of Valladolid, Spain
March 2026

This volume presents a single claim: Darwinian free-market optimization is a universal mechanism for the emergence of complexity in open-ended systems. The claim is supported by five independent domains—macroeconomic forecasting, prebiotic chemistry, chemical synthesis planning, language modelling, and adaptive immunity—in which the same algorithm, with the same 18 mechanisms, produces qualitatively correct behaviour without domain-specific fitting.

The Free-Market Algorithm (FMA) was not designed as a universal framework. At the 2008 financial crisis, while I was shifting from semiconductor to chemistry atomistic modelling, I started thinking about simulating economic activities. In 2017 I finally began—but deliberately chose not to read the existing literature, following instead my naive outsider common sense. In 2020 I uploaded to arXiv my first agent-based draft. I did not yet know about Agent-Based Models as a field, or even about input-output tables and Social Accounting Matrices.

The first surprise was to realise that the same code that deployed an economic system could be used to drive a robot carrying out several unrelated tasks in parallel, simply by asking the virtual settler workers to specialise on a specific task step as the "product" of their firm. The algorithm was therefore not just for economics—it could serve as a more general optimisation framework.

The second surprise came when I heard about input-output tables and saw how the algorithm was able to reproduce real-world economic data—not just academic insight exercises. The current version can reproduce macroeconomic time series from nothing more than a single input-output table. No time series, no estimated parameters, no expectations formation. The solution was to let autonomous agents discover transformation rules, trade goods, open and close firms, and compete for demand, with no centralised controller. The economy that emerged was not programmed; it self-organised through supply-and-demand dynamics, and it happened to forecast GDP with a mean absolute error comparable to the best professional institutions.

The recent introduction of AI assistants has made it possible to test the algorithm in fields where I have had no training. The next surprise came in 2025, when the same algorithm—unchanged—was applied to prebiotic chemistry. Starting from bare C, H, O, and N atoms, it discovered all 12 feasible amino acid formulas, all 5 canonical nucleobases, the complete formose sugar chain from formaldehyde to ribose, and Krebs cycle intermediates. No target molecules were specified. The algorithm treated atoms as goods, reactions as firms, and thermodynamic favourability as market demand. It worked because the problem had the same structure: open-ended search in a combinatorial space, guided by local fitness signals, with no global objective function.

A further surprise was that the FMA's internal dynamics matched, quantitatively, the signatures predicted by Assembly Theory for the boundary between random chemistry and selection-driven complexity. Three dynamical regimes emerged naturally—stagnation, selection, and tar—from varying a single parameter (temperature), and the transition points aligned with AT's theoretical thresholds. The FMA provided the constructive mechanism that AT's framework describes abstractly.

The next domain was language modelling. The FMA's market dynamics—where agents compete for demand, specialise through experience, and are replaced when unprofitable—map directly onto the Mixture-of-Experts architecture in modern transformers. To test this, I built nanoFMT, a minimal language model written from scratch in which there is no gradient descent: instead, each "expert" is a firm in a four-layer market hierarchy, and the training/inference dichotomy reduces to a single step: every forward pass is simultaneously learning and producing output, just as every market transaction simultaneously allocates resources and generates price signals. The polysemanticity phenomenon observed in mechanistic interpretability—where single neurons encode multiple unrelated features—emerges naturally from FMA's census coverage mechanism when the expert pool is small.

The most recent domain is adaptive immunity. Every named FMA mechanism has a precise immunological counterpart: birth/death maps to clonal expansion and contraction, the Evans-Polanyi activation barrier maps to lymphocyte activation thresholds, the depth penalty maps to T-cell exhaustion, the persistent recipe book maps to immunological memory, and census coverage maps to IL-7-driven homeostatic proliferation. The three regimes—stagnation, selection, and tar—correspond to immune tolerance, normal adaptive immunity, and cytokine storm, respectively. The mapping generates six novel falsifiable predictions, including that exhaustion scales with cascade depth (not antigen dose) and that cytokine storm severity is predicted by birth/death ratio (not IL-6 levels alone).

These are not analogies. The word "analogy" implies two separate systems that happen to resemble each other. What we observe is stronger: the same mechanism, operating under the same equations, producing the same dynamical signatures, in systems that share no substrate, no scale, and no evolutionary history. A macroeconomic firm and a B lymphocyte have nothing in common—except that both are autonomous agents competing in an open-ended market, and both are governed by the same 18 rules.

The Orchestration Principle

A pattern emerges across these five domains that deserves to be stated explicitly. In every case, FMA does not replace the native optimization of the domain. Instead, it operates one level above it, orchestrating the structural units that the domain's own dynamics cannot self-organize:

DomainNative optimizationFMA orchestrates
Macroeconomics Individual transactions (supply meets demand) Firms: creation, specialization, destruction
Prebiotic chemistry Individual reactions (thermodynamic favourability) Species populations: which molecules persist and grow
Language models Gradient descent (parameter optimization) Expert modules: birth, death, specialization, routing
Adaptive immunity Antigen-receptor binding (molecular recognition) Clonal populations: expansion, contraction, memory

This is the orchestration principle: FMA is not a competitor to gradient descent, thermodynamic selection, or antigen-receptor affinity. It is the layer that manages the population of problem-solvers, deciding which ones are born, which survive, which specialize, and which are retired. Gradient descent optimizes within an architecture; FMA optimizes the architecture itself. The same relationship holds in every domain: the local optimizer handles individual interactions, while FMA handles the population dynamics of the agents performing those interactions.

The adaptive immune system did not read Hayek. It discovered the free market by natural selection, running the same algorithm that governs macroeconomic coordination and prebiotic molecular assembly. That five domains as distant as national economies, primordial chemistry, robotic task planning, neural network architectures, and the vertebrate immune system converge on the same 18-mechanism structure is the central finding of this work, and the rest of the volume is the evidence.
